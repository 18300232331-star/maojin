Hotel Towel Delivery Inspection: How to Check Your Towels on Arrival

2026-11-27 · Product Knowledge

When a bulk towel delivery arrives, count the cartons and pieces against the packing list, pull a sample using the sampling table in section 2, and check each sampled towel for weight, size, colour against the approved sample, stitching and moisture before you sign. Most delivery problems are cheapest to fix in the first 30 minutes, while the driver is still there.

1. Before the truck arrives: 10 minutes of preparation

An inspection done well starts before the cartons are opened. Put three things on the table: the packing list, the order confirmation, and the approved sample — the actual towel you approved, not a photo of it. That sample is the standard the delivery is judged against, so keep it where you can lay a delivered towel next to it. You need four tools: a tape measure, a scale that reads in grams, a pen for the delivery note, and a phone camera. Check the towels in daylight or under a bright white light, and write down what you find as you go: a short count remembered an hour later is a dispute; written on the delivery note with a photo, it is a fact. Our five checks before you approve a towel sample guide covers that sample check; this guide starts where it ends, at the warehouse door.

2. The numbers: how many towels to pull

You cannot open every carton and unfold every towel. Bulk textile inspection works on sampling: you pull a fixed number of towels at random from across the whole delivery, and the defective count in that sample decides whether the lot is accepted. The standard tool is the published AQL sampling table — AQL stands for acceptance quality limit — and the plan buyers most commonly use is General Inspection Level II, normal inspection, single sampling, AQL 2.5 for major defects. Level II is the default unless the contract names another one.

Find your lot size, take the code letter and sample size, then inspect that many towels picked at random from different cartons, layers, colours and sizes. At or below the "accept" number, accept the lot; at the "reject" number, hold it and talk to the supplier before signing. Agree the inspection level and AQL value before the goods ship; the values above are the normal single-sampling plans of the ISO 2859-1 scheme, a rule for a continuing series of lots, not a guarantee about any single towel — which is why the checks below matter as well.

3. Count first: cartons, pieces, colours and sizes

Counting comes first: it cannot be fixed after the driver leaves. Count the cartons as they come off the truck and compare the total with the packing list. Check each carton label against the list before opening it. Then open at least 1 carton of every colour and size and count the pieces: 3 colours and 2 sizes means 6 cartons opened and counted, not 1.

If the count is short, do not sign for the full quantity. Write the exact shortage on the delivery note — "received 480 pieces of 500 ordered, bath towel white 70×140 cm" — photograph the carton labels and the counted stack, and tell the supplier the same day. A short count found at delivery is a shipping error; found a week later, after towels have been put into circulation, it is a dispute nobody wins. Our hotel towel minimum order quantity guide sets out the order sizes this site works to.

4. Weigh and measure: the GSM spot check

Weight is the fastest way to catch a towel that is lighter than ordered, and it needs no lab. GSM means grams per square metre, so a towel's weight is its size multiplied by its GSM: a 70×140 cm bath towel measures 0.98 square metres, so at 600 GSM it should weigh 588 g — arithmetic from the live product page's own published spec for our hotel bath towel 70 by 140 cm. As a worked example, a 70×140 cm towel at 500 GSM should weigh about 490 g; that is an example, not a product spec. Weigh 5 towels from the sample and average them; one towel can vary, but the average of 5 tells you what the lot really weighs. If the average is clearly below the weight the size and GSM imply, flag it before signing: a light towel is a thin towel, and every guest will feel it.

Measure size the same way, tape measure and approved sample side by side. Lay each sampled towel flat, smooth out the folds without stretching it, and measure length and width against the approved sample: a bath towel is 70×140 cm and a bath sheet 80×160 cm in our hotel towel sizes guide, and a towel 5 cm short will not fold to the size your shelves and trolleys are set for. See our GSM explained: what 400, 500 and 600 really mean guide and the hotel bath towel weight chart for how weight bands feel in use, and our absorbency testing: the 3-second sink test on every batch guide for the 3-second sink test at the warehouse sink.

5. Colour and look: compare with the approved sample, in daylight

Colour is where most delivery disputes start. Lay a delivered towel flat next to the approved sample in daylight and look at both: the two should match — not "close in this light", but the same colour in the same light. Check the dye is even across the whole towel — no pale patches, streaks or darker edges — and check every colour in the order, not just the first one: dye lots vary, and the second colour in the stack is the one that gets missed. If the towels carry a logo, check its placement against the approved sample too: a logo stitched 2 cm off centre shows on every towel.

The approved sample shows the colour and hand-feel of the production lot, but not how the colour will hold after months of hotel washing: that depends on the dye method. Ask for the dye-lot test results with the delivery — wash-fastness and rubbing-fastness from the actual production lot, not a value from a similar cloth. Our reactive-dyed colours survive 100 hotel washes — see our reactive dyeing: colour that survives 100 hotel washes guide. If the colour is off, do not wash a whole carton to "see if it comes right": photograph the delivered towel next to the approved sample in daylight, keep both, and send the photo to the supplier that day.

6. Stitching and defects: what counts as major

Open each sampled towel fully and look at it the way a guest will: both faces, hems, selvedges and corners. Look for holes, tears, stains, dye spots, pulled loops, uneven pile, loose hemming, foreign fibres. Sort what you find into two groups before you count. Major defects are ones a guest would notice or that shorten the towel's life: a hole, a stain, a wrong size, a hem coming undone, a large pulled loop. Minor defects are small blemishes a guest would not notice and that do not affect use, such as a single loose thread end that trims off cleanly. Count the major defects against the sampling table in section 2; note the minor ones separately, but do not let a handful of loose thread ends reject a lot that is otherwise right.

First, inspect the towels in the order you pull them, not after sorting them into a "good" pile and a "bad" pile — sorting first is how a bad sample turns into a good one by accident. Second, keep every defective towel you find, tagged with the carton it came from: if the lot is held, the supplier needs to see the actual defects, not a description of them. See our when to replace hotel towels guide for the same inspection points at the end of a towel's life.

7. Packaging and moisture: the check buyers skip

Moisture is the quietest delivery problem and the most expensive: a damp towel stacked and stored will go musty and can grow mildew before anyone opens the carton again. Feel the outside of every carton as it comes off the truck: one that feels soft, swollen or damp has been wet, and one that smells musty has been wet longer. Open any damp carton and check the towels inside for water marks, a musty smell and dark spots on the folds. Do not sign for damp cartons as if they were dry, and do not stack damp towels with dry ones: spread them out to air, photograph them, and tell the supplier the same day.

A towel can feel damp without being visibly wet because cotton holds water by nature. Published textile reference tables give cotton's standard commercial moisture regain as 8.5%, and the standard test atmosphere for textiles is 65% relative humidity at 20°C. In plain terms, a cotton towel in normal air already carries about 8.5% of its dry weight as water, and a carton stored above 65–70% relative humidity picks up more and may grow mildew. Towels should therefore arrive in dry, sealed cartons, inner bags intact, stored off the floor. Our how we pack 500 bath towels for sea freight guide shows how the cartons should look when they leave the factory — crushed corners, torn bags or wet board mean the packing is part of the finding, not just the towels.

8. Decide: accept, hold, or reject — and write it down

At the end of the checks you have three options, and only three. Accept the lot when the count matches the packing list, the sample is inside the accept number, the weight and size match the approved sample, and the cartons are dry: sign the delivery note, file the photos and sample counts with the order, and put the towels into stock. Hold the lot when something is wrong but fixable: a short count, defects over the accept number, a colour close but not matching, or a damp carton. Quarantine it — apart from good stock, cartons tagged, everything photographed — and message the supplier the same day with the numbers, not just "there is a problem". Reject the lot only when the problem is not fixable on the spot: a count badly short of the order, defects through most of the sample, a colour clearly a different shade, or damp and mould through several cartons. Write the finding on the delivery note itself — "held, 6 defectives in a sample of 80, photographs attached" — and keep a copy.

Frequently asked questions

How many towels should I inspect from a bulk delivery? Use the sampling table in section 2: for a lot of 501–1,200 pieces, inspect 80 towels picked at random and accept the lot on 5 defectives or fewer. For 281–500 pieces the sample is 50 towels, for 1,201–3,200 pieces 125. A sample from the top carton only tells you about the top carton.

What should I do if the count is short? Do not sign for the full quantity. Write the exact shortage on the delivery note, photograph the carton labels and the counted stack, and tell the supplier the same day. A short count found at delivery is a shipping error; found a week later, after towels have been put into circulation, it is a dispute nobody wins.

My towels weigh less than the approved sample — is that a problem? Check the arithmetic first: a 70×140 cm towel at 600 GSM weighs 588 g, and at 500 GSM about 490 g. Weigh 5 towels and average them — one towel can vary, but the average of 5 shows what the lot really weighs. If the average is clearly below the weight the size and GSM imply, flag it before signing, with the 5 weights written down.

What if the colour is different from the approved sample? Compare the delivered towel and the approved sample side by side in daylight. If they do not match, photograph both together, keep both towels, and send the photo to the supplier the same day. Do not wash a whole carton to see if the colour comes right; ask for the dye-lot test results from the actual production lot.

Can I reject a delivery for a few defective towels? Only if the defects in your sample reach the reject number — for example, 6 or more in a sample of 80. A few loose thread ends are minor and do not reject a lot; a hole, a stain or a hem coming undone is major and counts. If the count reaches the reject number, hold the lot, quarantine it, and talk to the supplier before signing.
