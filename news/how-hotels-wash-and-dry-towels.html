How Hotels Wash and Dry Towels: Temperature, Detergent and Drying Guide

2026-11-26 · Product Knowledge

Wash white hotel towels at 60°C, coloured towels at 40°C and dark or vivid colours at 30°C, use about half the detergent the packet suggests and no fabric softener, then tumble dry on low or medium heat until fully dry. That routine — the right temperature for the colour, a small detergent dose and a complete dry — is what keeps a cotton towel soft and absorbent through 100 hotel washes instead of going stiff, grey or musty in the first 20.

1. The routine in one pass: sort, wash, dry, fold

A hotel laundry runs the same four steps every cycle, and the order matters more than any single setting. First, sort: whites together, light colours together, dark and vivid colours together, and pool and beach towels apart from room towels — pool towels carry chlorine and sunscreen, and dark towels shed dye onto whites. Wash towels on their own, not mixed with sheets or guest clothing, so zips, buttons and hooks cannot snag the loops and the load has room to rinse. Second, wash at the temperature the table in section 2 gives for that load. Third, move towels to the dryer promptly — a wet towel left sitting in the drum for even an hour starts to smell, and mildew once established is hard to wash out. Fourth, dry fully, shake each towel out as it leaves the dryer to open the loops, and fold only when completely dry. A towel folded even slightly damp will smell musty in the cupboard within a day, and that smell is what guests notice first. Our how to store hotel towels guide covers the storage half of this routine in detail.

2. The numbers: wash temperature by towel type

Temperature is the one setting buyers argue about most, and the published guides agree on it more than the argument suggests. The split below is the consensus across four independent towel-washing guides, and it balances three things at once: cleaning power, colour retention and fibre life. Hot water above 60°C sanitises and dissolves body oils, but it also weakens cotton fibres and causes shrinkage if used on every load — so the hot cycle is a tool for whites and heavy soil, not a default.

Two notes belong with this table. First, the 71°C for 25 minutes row is the thermal-disinfection standard in published healthcare laundry guidance (US CDC), adopted by some hotels for soiled linen where disinfection is required — it is not the everyday hotel setting, and most hotel loads never need it. The same guidance gives a low-temperature alternative: 22–25°C with a 125 ppm chlorine bleach rinse. Second, chlorine bleach itself is activated at 57–63°C, with a 50–150 ppm residual in the bleach cycle — which is why a 60°C white wash and a measured bleach dose work together, and why bleach in a cold wash does far less. Coloured towels should never meet chlorine bleach at any temperature: use oxygen bleach, which is colour-safe. If your whites are greying despite a 60°C wash, the cause is usually detergent residue or hard water, not temperature — see our white towels turning grey: laundry causes and fixes guide.

3. Detergent: how much, and what to leave out

The most common laundry mistake in hotels is using too much detergent, not too little. Towels are not heavily soiled — they need rinsing more than scrubbing — and every published guide in our sources agrees on the same dose: about half the amount the packet suggests for the load size. Excess detergent stays trapped in the loops, and that residue is what makes a towel stiff, dull and less absorbent. Liquid detergent dissolves more easily than powder at lower temperatures and leaves less residue behind, which matters most on the 30°C dark-colour cycle. Fill the drum only about three-quarters full, so the towels have room to move and rinse clean; a packed drum cannot rinse, whatever the dose.

Fabric softener should be skipped entirely. It coats the fibres in a waxy film that feels soft for one wash and then steadily kills absorbency — the towel stops taking up water because the loops are sealed. Dryer sheets do the same thing and should be skipped for the same reason. Two cheap additives do the job softener pretends to do: half a cup, about 120 ml, of white vinegar in the rinse cycle dissolves detergent residue and mineral deposits and neutralises odour, and half a cup of baking soda in the drum with the detergent neutralises odours and brightens fibres. For a towel that has already gone stiff from residue, run one hot 60°C cycle with a cup of white vinegar and no detergent, then a normal 40°C wash with half the usual dose — the full recovery routine is in our bath towel getting stiff? It's probably these three habits guide.

4. Drying: tumble low, never over-dry, finish fully dry

A tumble dryer is the easiest way to get a fluffy finish, as long as the heat stays low. Use low or medium heat and take towels out as soon as they are dry: high heat damages cotton loops, bakes any detergent residue into the fibres and makes the towel rough. Over-drying is the second most common mistake after over-dosing detergent — it makes cotton fibres brittle. Add 2 clean tennis balls or wool dryer balls to the drum: they beat the loops open, fluff the pile and speed up drying. If you line dry, towels can feel stiff from the breeze; 10 minutes in the dryer afterwards restores the softness. Waffle-weave towels dry 40–50% faster than terry at the same size, which is one reason spas and gyms favour them — see our GSM explained: what 400, 500 and 600 really mean guide for how weight and weave change drying time. Whatever the method, the rule is the same: a towel is not dry until it is dry all the way through, including the folded centre. Pool towels are the heaviest load in the hotel laundry — a wet 70×140 cm towel at 600 GSM weighs 588 g dry and far more wet — so give them a full medium-heat cycle and never fold them damp; our pool towels for hotels guide covers their sizing and weight.

5. Five mistakes that age a towel before its time

First, overloading the drum: a drum filled past three-quarters cannot rinse, so detergent stays in the loops and the towel stiffens within a few washes. Second, fabric softener on every load: the waxy coating builds up wash after wash until the towel barely absorbs. Third, high dryer heat: it feels faster, but it cooks the loops and shortens the towel's life. Fourth, leaving wet towels in the washer: even an hour is enough for a musty smell to start, and repeated damp storage is the fastest route to a towel a guest will complain about. Fifth, washing new towels with old ones and skipping the first-wash routine: new towels shed lint for the first 3 washes, and that lint settles on older towels and in the dryer filter. Wash new towels separately at 30–40°C for the first 3 washes, with half the detergent and no softener, and the lint problem mostly disappears. A towel that has already lost its softness or thinned at the hems is not a laundry problem any more — it is a replacement decision, and our when to replace hotel towels guide gives the inspection points.

6. What to ask for before you order

A towel's laundry behaviour is decided before it is woven, so put it in the inquiry. Ask for the wash-fastness and rubbing-fastness results from the actual production lot, not a datasheet value from a similar cloth — fastness results vary by shade and construction, so ask for the results rather than a single number. Ask how the towel was dyed: our reactive-dyed colours survive 100 hotel washes, and the dye method is what stands between a white towel and a grey one — see our reactive dyeing: colour that survives 100 hotel washes guide. Ask for a sample and wash it 5 times in your own laundry before approving bulk production: a towel that feels perfect on arrival can change after 5 washes, and that is the version your guests will actually use. State the size in the inquiry too — a bath towel is 70×140 cm and a bath sheet 80×160 cm in our hotel towel sizes guide — because a heavier towel costs more to dry per cycle, and the laundry cost belongs in the buying decision, not after it.

Frequently asked questions

What temperature should hotels wash white towels at? 60°C for the regular white load, with a normal detergent dose and oxygen bleach. That temperature sanitises, dissolves body oils and keeps whites bright, and all four published towel-washing guides in our sources agree on it. Reserve 71°C for 25 minutes for thermal disinfection only where your laundry protocol requires it.

Can coloured hotel towels be washed at 60°C? They can, but they should not be washed that hot every time: 40°C for light colours and 30°C for dark or vivid colours keeps the colour in and the fibres intact. If a coloured load needs a deeper clean, use oxygen bleach at 40°C rather than raising the temperature.

Why do hotel towels go stiff after a few washes? Almost always detergent residue, fabric softener or over-drying — not the towel itself. Use half the usual detergent dose, skip softener entirely, dry on low or medium heat and take towels out as soon as they are dry. A vinegar strip wash (one 60°C cycle with a cup of white vinegar and no detergent) usually restores a stiff towel.

Should hotels use fabric softener on towels? No. Softener coats the cotton loops in a waxy film that reduces absorbency with every wash. Use half a cup of white vinegar in the rinse cycle instead — it dissolves residue, softens the fibres naturally and removes odour without coating them.

How should hotels dry towels — tumble dryer or line? Tumble dry on low or medium heat for the softest result, with 2 dryer balls in the drum, and never over-dry. Line drying works and saves energy, but towels dried on the line feel stiff; 10 minutes in the dryer afterwards restores the fluff. Either way, fold only when the towel is dry all the way through.
