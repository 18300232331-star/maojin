Custom-Dyed Hotel Towels: Colour Matching, Lab Dips and Minimums

2026-11-22 · Product Knowledge

A custom colour is decided before bulk production, not after the cartons arrive. The mill dyes a small lab-dip swatch of your actual towel, you approve it, and only then does the bulk dyeing start — and any custom-dyed product takes 500 pieces per colour, inside the US$500 minimum order. Skip the lab dip to save time, and the "same" colour on the re-order is a gamble.

1. The two numbers that decide the order

Two numbers decide whether a custom colour is possible at all. The first is the per-colour minimum: 500 pieces per colour for any custom-dyed product. The second is the order minimum: US$500 for the whole order, including samples, with no exceptions. Both must be met — one colour of bath towels is 500 pieces, two colours is 1,000 pieces, and whether US$500 is met is confirmed on the quotation. Our hotel towel minimum order quantity guide gives the full table by product.

The per-colour minimum exists because dyeing happens in batches. A dye machine is loaded, the recipe is mixed, and the whole batch is dyed at once — a 100-piece run costs nearly as much dyeing time as a 500-piece run, and the colour cannot be topped up later in the same shade. That is also why a re-order is never exactly the same shade as the first order: dyeing happens in batches, and small differences in fibre, water and process shift every batch slightly. No colour process makes two separate lots identical. What separates a disciplined mill from a careless one is not zero variation — it is whether the variation is measured, bounded by a written tolerance, and checked against a sealed physical standard before the order ships.

2. The four approval stages

Colour approval runs in stages, and each stage proves something different. Do not approve a colour on a paper colour chip alone — cotton yarn, terry texture and finishing chemicals can all shift the shade you see.

StageWhat it isWhat it proves
1. Lab dipA small swatch, a few centimetres of the actual yarn or fabric, dyed to a proposed recipe — usually two or three options submittedThe dye formula and the target shade on your real substrate
2. Yarn-dyed cone or hank sampleColoured yarn dyed before weavingHow the colour behaves where the border yarn is dyed before weaving
3. Strike-off or handloom sampleA sample in the actual constructionHow the colour looks in the real terry weave, not on a flat swatch
4. Pre-production sampleMade on production equipment, with washing and finishing close to bulk conditionsThat the bulk run will match what you approved

The lab dip is the cheapest place in the whole chain to fix a colour, which is why skipping it to save time is a false economy. Approving it locks down the recipe on that substrate, judged under the agreed light source, at that moment. Read the scope of that approval honestly: a lab dip does not promise that the production machine will reproduce it exactly. A laboratory beaker and a production dyeing machine differ in liquor flow, heat-up rate and dye liquor ratio, and scaled-up dyeing routinely shifts the shade by a small but visible amount. That is what the pre-production sample is for — it catches the shift before the bulk run, not after.

Set the target with a physical swatch, a Pantone textile reference or spectral data. Keep the approved lab dip sealed as the physical standard for the bulk run: every dye lot is measured against it and released, corrected or rejected. If the property re-orders in six months, the same sealed standard is the reference again — not a memory of the shade, and not a photograph.

3. Why reactive dyeing, and what fastness means

For cotton hotel towels, reactive dyes are the standard choice, because they bond chemically with the fibre and give good wash fastness when properly fixed and washed off. The exact dye choice depends on the shade depth, the fastness required and the cost target — dark shades and pale shades do not behave the same way, and a dark border next to a white body is the highest-risk combination, because the colour can migrate where the two meet.

Colour fastness is the towel's ability to keep its original shade under normal use and care. For a hotel towel the checks that matter most are wash fastness, rubbing fastness and, for pool and spa towels, light fastness and chlorine resistance. Ask the supplier for the results before you approve the bulk run: which test method was used, how many wash cycles were simulated, what detergent and temperature were used, and whether the result is from the body fabric, the border or the jacquard area. A test on virgin fabric is not the same as a test after washing. Our reactive dyeing colour that survives 100 hotel washes guide explains the dyeing side in full; this article is about what to ask for, not what grade to expect — fastness grades vary by shade and construction, and any grade quoted without the test conditions behind it is meaningless.

The laundry matters as much as the dye. A towel in a hotel laundry faces high-alkalinity detergent, oxygen bleach and tumble drying — a different stress from a towel washed at home. Tell the supplier it is a hotel towel, and ask whether the dye system suits repeated hotel laundering and oxygen bleach.

4. Judging the colour: light, tolerance and metamerism

Colour is judged by eye in a light booth and measured with a spectrophotometer. The primary source is D65 average daylight at 6,500 K; the match is also checked under TL84 store fluorescent at 4,100 K and under A incandescent at 2,856 K. The difference between the sample and the standard is reported as ΔE, most often by the CMC (2:1) formula, with a tolerance of 1.0 common — tighter for critical shades.

The trap is metamerism: a pair can match under one light and break apart under another, because different dye recipes were used. Two towels that agree in the hotel lobby can disagree in the bathroom. Checking under two or more light sources catches it, which is why the approval should name the light sources, not just the shade. If your property's lighting is warm white, say so — a colour approved under daylight alone may not be the colour your guests see.

5. What to send with your inquiry

Send the target shade as a physical swatch or a Pantone textile reference — not a screen photograph, which no mill can match reliably. Send the product and size from the hotel towel sizes guide, the quantity per colour (500 pieces minimum per colour), and whether the towel is for a hotel laundry, a spa or a pool, so the dye system is chosen for the right washing conditions. Ask for the lab dip first, then the pre-production sample, and ask what wash-fastness and rubbing-fastness results the mill will supply with the sample.

If you also need a logo, decide the method with the colour: our logo towels — embroidery, jacquard or print, which lasts guide compares the three, and a jacquard logo in a custom colour has its own minimums and lead time. And confirm the whole order against the US$500 minimum — samples count toward it, so a lab dip and a pre-production sample do not sit outside the order, they sit inside it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the minimum for custom-dyed towels? 500 pieces per colour for any custom-dyed product, inside a US$500 minimum order that includes samples. One colour of bath towels is 500 pieces; two colours is 1,000 pieces.

What is a lab dip? A small swatch of your actual yarn or fabric, dyed to a proposed recipe and submitted for approval before bulk production. Usually two or three options are submitted and you approve one. It is the cheapest place to fix a colour.

Will the re-order match the first order exactly? No colour process makes two separate dye lots identical. A disciplined mill measures the variation against the sealed approved lab dip, keeps it inside the agreed tolerance — commonly ΔE 1.0 — and checks it before shipping.

Can I approve a colour from a paper colour chip? No. Cotton yarn, terry texture and finishing chemicals shift the shade. Approve on the lab dip in the actual yarn or fabric, judged under the agreed light sources.

Does custom dyeing change the towel's washing performance? The dye system must suit hotel laundering. Reactive dyes bond chemically with cotton and give good wash fastness when properly fixed and washed off. Ask for the wash-fastness and rubbing-fastness results, and say whether the towel faces oxygen bleach in the hotel laundry.
